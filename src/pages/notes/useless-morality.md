I don't know if I'd describe myself as an "effective altruist", but I definitely believe in the words literally - that it's important not just to be altruistic, but to be effective.

Over the years I've seen lots of very strong opinions opposed to the movement, mostly on the basis of its popular members, but often on the concept alone. I attended a "drunken philosophy" event in Seattle a few years back where I heard this kind of sentiment.

When I press people personally, it's really hard to get a coherent argument out of them. Any position opposed to the literal meaning just seems terribly off.

Put plainly, the idea is to do good in ways that work. To analyze the mechanisms of doing good and tweak them to work better. To understand what matters and put our energy into that work more than into the rest.

I read a thread the other day (which I've been struggling to find a link to, sorry) that put the opposition in terms that made sense to me. The objection is not to the results, but to the very act of calculating moral worth. To many, the idea of engaging in moral mathematics is itself immoral.

This clears up a lot for me. I'm used to this kind of thinking.

Growing up, this sounded like the opposition to same-sex relationships. I heard it was "wrong" and "bad" and "ugly" on its merits alone. It was immoral for no reason outside of itself. This confused me then, and luckily it still does as an adult.

You hear many arguments of the same flavor for all sorts of things. Why a war is justified. Why we should have nuclear families. Why "respect" in our culture is the correct form of respect universally.

It's all useless morality.

When you break from a deep religious fold, it does quite some damage to your conception of the world. It took me a long time to build myself back up. The framework I based moral worth around had completely crumbled, and I didn't know what else to look toward.

The upside is that it makes you confront deep and obvious philosophical questions around behavior and value. I could not call things immoral without evidence. I couldn't even frame an objective moral reality.

What you fall into quite quickly is the concept of relative morality. With a few building blocks of a moral framework, you can deduce what else is immoral.

There's no easy way to do this, but mine looked something like this:

Your responsibility is to act reasonably as part of a whole. Killing, stealing, hurting - this all damages the whole more than it heals it. You get less value than the damage you cause.

These are all "ought" statements, which any philosopher knows cannot come from an "is". In truth, the only real basis for a framework is evolutionary psychology, but even this is not something you want to base an "ought" on.

For the goal of living in a society that you would enjoy, there are oughts. You ought to be kind. You ought to help. You ought to be effective.

Given many options, some of which are less effective, you ought to choose the more effective.

Anything else is useless morality.
